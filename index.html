01 · Why Gerontology & Nutritional Science

I used to think gerontology wasn't for me — studying aging, or studying for elderly people, didn't pull me in. I was wrong about why. Aging isn't a subject that starts at 65; the clock starts at birth. What actually interested me wasn't decline. It was time itself — and as medicine gets better at extending the body, what happens to the mind across those extra years matters just as much. How to make more of that time healthy, in body and mind, and less of it sick.

My two biggest projects sit at opposite ends of a single lifespan. Lumi's Nutrition Lab teaches second graders to read their own bodies. Hyodo Bapsang feeds and sits with people in their eighties. Inflammaging — the slow accumulation that links a child's first habits to an elder's last years — is the thread that runs between them. But it isn't the only one.

The deeper thread is where I think real prevention actually happens: not in a prescription written after something has already gone wrong, but in the three meals eaten before it's ever needed. And not in a guideline built for an average body, but in learning to read your own. Lumi's rule is that no two readers get the same result, and I felt nothing counts as real data too — the same standard I hold my own research to, documenting counter-evidence instead of only what confirms the hypothesis. That instinct, the individual over the average, is what precision nutrition is built on — and I think it has to start young. Healthspan isn't built at eighty. It's built by whoever learns, early, to read their own body honestly.

The most specific thing I have to bring to this is Korean fermentation. A hypothesis from Professor Park Sang-chul got me started: Korea's long-lived elders, on traditional diets nearly free of meat, rarely show B12 deficiency — and traditional fermented soy foods may be why. The Mediterranean diet took decades of research to become the West's model for longevity. Korean fermented food could be next. Almost no one has tested that rigorously yet. I want to be one of the people who does.

02 · Research

Five studies, one question: what slows the clock?

Aug 2026

Cognitive Healthspan and the Gut-Brain Axis: A Scoping Review of Korean Traditional Fermented Soy Foods

Korea's healthy life expectancy trails its overall life expectancy by 18 years. This scoping review maps existing evidence connecting Korea's traditional fermented soy foods to gut microbial composition and, through the gut–brain axis, to cognitive outcomes in aging populations. It's positioned against a 2023 update to the hallmarks-of-aging framework, which added gut dysbiosis and chronic inflammation as recognized drivers of aging — making the diet–microbiota–brain pathway a candidate prevention strategy worth mapping. I applied an explicit source-verification protocol, separating primary sources I examined directly from those cited secondhand, and documented counter-evidence rather than selecting only for confirming findings.

Sept 2026 · published, Journal of High School Research, Vol. 3 No. 1

Dietary Management as a Strategy for Reducing Inflammaging in Aging Populations: A Literature Review (read full text)

Inflammaging — the chronic, low-grade inflammation that accumulates with age — is increasingly treated as a driver of aging rather than a side effect. This review synthesizes nutritional and gerontological research on dietary interventions that target it.

Okra Mucilage & Calcium Alginate Beads — July 2026

Tested whether okra mucilage improves a calcium-alginate bead's ability to capture microplastic-sized particles in a simulated gut. In final review, Journal of Emerging Investigators.

Aspartame vs. Sucrose — January 2026

A literature-based comparison of their metabolic effects. Published, Journal of Research High School.

Nano-plastic Exposure in Hot Food & Beverages — 2025

Surveyed 203 people on what drives cumulative exposure; developed into a venture proposal for the Blue Ocean and Diamond Challenge competitions.

Fermentation and Aging: Field Observation Report & Proposal — May 2026

03 · Applied Innovation

When a problem needs a tool, not just a paper

Chitosan–tea polyphenol edible coating

Tested against uncoated produce across strawberries, cherry tomatoes, and apples, tracking moisture loss, color, and mold over seven days. The effect was clearest in strawberries — about 65% less moisture loss under refrigeration than untreated fruit — and weaker in thicker-skinned produce like apple. As EcoShield, it won Champion at the 2026 BIEA STEM Competition (certificate & photos), was presented at the IEOM International Conference, and won at the Global Innovation Challenge. It's now being commercialized through FV Lab, with a patent application filed in October 2026 with two other student co-inventors.

Image-based fencing blade corrosion grading

My blade snapped mid-bout at a fencing semifinal in June 2024 — my third spare, gone, and I finished the match on a borrowed blade that never felt like mine. Gathering the pieces afterward, I found rust spreading out from the core of the break: the surface had looked flawless all season while sweat and humidity worked at the steel from the inside, unseen. Blades are graded by eye, which means the call on whether one is safe to use depends on who's looking. I designed the capture protocol and grading rubric, shot 50 rusted blades from my fencing club to build the reference set, and worked with a professional developer to build the tool — now live as FerroTrace. It's filed as a provisional patent with the Korea Intellectual Property Office.

04 · Human Health in Practice

Hyodo Bapsang

Hyodo is filial piety — the duty and care owed to one's parents and elders. Bapsang is a properly set dining table. Together, Hyodo Bapsang means something close to a table of respect: a meal prepared with care, offered as a basic right rather than a charity. Mapo-gu launched the program in 2023 with a single eligibility rule — resident, 75 or older, no income test — and in 2025 the ASEM Global Ageing Center named it a best-practice model for community-based elder care.

In May 2024, I founded a youth volunteer corps to support it. It started with six members; it's twelve now. On-site meal service runs on weekday afternoons, so I'm there during school breaks and around the Lunar New Year holiday — plating meals, distributing them, sitting with people while they eat.

On semester weekends, when on-site service isn't possible, what grew alongside it was: seven busking sessions with the classical ensemble at Mapo Red Road — turnout varied each time, drawn from whoever in the corps could make it — each one used to explain the program to passersby and collect donations on the spot; a performance for residents at the "Yeonnam-dong Very Small Concert"; a logo and promotional poster I designed. Mapo-gu has recognized the work three times since 2024, most recently a mayoral Letter of Appreciation in August 2026.

I also wrote the program up formally — Multidimensional Value Analysis of a Communal Dining Policy: Hyodo Bapsang in a Super-Aged Society, an independent analysis across nutritional, economic, environmental, and sociocultural value that argues for nationwide adoption of the model. It's mentioned in the Mapo Welfare Foundation's 2025 Annual Report.

My paper pushes past the nutrition angle, too — it argues the shared table works as a "factory of trust," the mechanism by which social isolation among the elderly actually gets reversed. That premise is what the number below tries to test.

I didn't set out to write a policy paper about a program I volunteer for. But once I'd spent school breaks plating rice at that dining hall, the question of whether it was actually worth the money stopped being academic.

05 · Performance as Advocacy

An orchestra that plays for a cause

I joined the Lee Sang-hee & Friends Youth Charity Orchestra in August 2022 — as principal violinist and soloist at first, concertmaster since August 2024, and, since March 2025, opening every concert from the stage as well. Most ensembles play for an audience; this one plays for a cause: every concert’s proceeds go entirely to its beneficiaries.

As an IVI Giving Messenger, I’ve played five of the International Vaccine Institute’s annual benefit concerts, funding vaccine development and distribution for children in developing countries; for the National Cancer Center’s series, four more, funding cancer research and patient support. The work has drawn commendations from the National Cancer Center, the city of Goyang, and a national student-volunteer competition in my own name, and in 2025, a Commendation from the Prime Minister of the Republic of Korea to the orchestra itself. IVI profiled it this year in a solo interview, “Until Our Music Becomes a Vaccine for the World’s Children.”

In January 2026, a fire in Guryong Village — one of Seoul’s last informal settlements — displaced more than 180 residents. I founded a 10-member ensemble of fellow international-school students to respond: “Music Heals, Hope Restores.” We played our first benefit concert that March; a second, Harmony for Nepal, followed in October 2026.

The violin doesn’t cure anything. But it’s gotten me into rooms — hospital wards, fundraising galas, a senior dining hall in Yeonnam-dong, a village rebuilding after a fire — that a research paper alone wouldn’t.

06 · Science Communication

Lumi's Nutrition Lab

Nutrition science trained me to distrust two things: food sorted into good and bad, and a result promised in advance. Reading the nutrition books already written for eight-year-olds, I found both anyway — vegetables as virtue, sugar as a verdict, a crash that plenty of real kids never feel. I wanted to write the version that doesn't do that: a story that treats a child as a scientist running her own experiment, where noticing nothing is as valid a result as noticing something dramatic.

The larger project is Lumi's Nutrition Lab: The Junior Scientist Series, a ten-volume series written mainly for second graders, developed under the mentorship of Baninoon's founder, Kim Jun-hee. Each book follows Jiwoo, a nine-year-old, through an ordinary situation that goes wrong in an interesting way — a sports drink before a match, popcorn with no water — alongside Lumi, an Inner-Body Data Collector who never scolds her, only records. Every volume ends on the same line: not every body responds the same way, and I felt nothing counts as real data too.

Lumi's face is Jiwoo's own, just older — the same round cheeks, the same hair, with glasses added. I drew her that way on purpose: less a mentor than a future self who came back, not to correct the child but to sit beside her and help her read her own body, because the person you grow into would never scold the person you were. I wrote her that way for second graders. It took me longer to notice I'd written her for myself, too.

Since January 2024, I've worked as Design Lead and Content Development Intern at Baninoon Co., a children's publisher — translating their picture book What Happened to Baninoon Who Would Not Eat into English, designing props for a KPIPA-funded musical kit, and eventually writing this series' manuscripts myself. I signed a publishing contract for the first three volumes in June 2025 — I kept the copyright; Baninoon holds print and distribution rights — and they were published this fall, after a pilot with six second graders in June 2026.

If the research is how I study nutrition science, this is how I'd explain it to someone seven years old and make them want to know more.
